\chapter{Quantitative Evaluation of Property-Based\\Testing}\label{chap:etna}

The second chapter of this thesis remarks a fragmentation caused by the design of
property-based testing libraries, leading to diverging features, separated ecosystems.
In this chapter, I focus on yet another fragmentation, this time caused by something
much more banal, a lack of general purpose evaluation benchmarks for PBT. The
literature is full of interesting novel techniques for enhancing random generation and
counterexample minimization, but these techniques are rarely evaluated within a
coherent context. Many times, the studies have relied on bespoke benchmarks
(\todo{examples}), if one were to build a web of studies and the benchmarks they used,
the result is somewhat reminiscent of the infamous public transport coverage of Ankara,
a small number of hub workloads that many studies reuse, many one-off benchmarks that
haven't been validated or reused in other studies, and a complete lack of
standardization.

This lack of common comparative analysis surely contributes to a lack of understanding
in the effects of these new techniques and changes; but the problem is persistent
beyond the literature and into the libraries themselves. All the property-based testing
libraries I have read in the course of developing this thesis are full of bespoke
decisions accummulated in the last 25 years. The choice of which sizing function to
use, the shrinking algorithm, the default settings on budgets and parameters... Many of
these decisions have never been subject to quantitative judgement beyond the initial
discussions, never been evaluated at scale, or understood in the broader context of PBT
usage.

In the beginning of my Ph.D. we set out to remediate this fragmentation and help build
a common quantitative understanding for the field. With Jessica and Harry implementing
the common evaluation framework in Python as well as the Haskell workloads, and I
working on the implementation of the Rocq pipeline and workloads, we have published
``Etna: An Evaluation Platform for Property-Based Testing (Experience
Report)''~\cite{etna-icfp23} in ICFP23.

After the initial publication, I have taken the lead role in future development of the
ETNA platform and horizontally grown it for OCaml with Nikhil Kamath, Racket with Ceren
Mert and Justine Frank, for Haskell with George Miao, and for Rust by myself. The
initial implementation as a library has been replaced with a standalone tool that could
be easily run cross-platform on many machines, eventually to the point we can start to
run our experiments as Github Actions. Many of these changes have found their ways into
publications, our JFP extension for the ICFP23 paper has incorporated the work by Ceren
and Nikhil as well as Tin from UPenn working together with Jessica.

The rest of this chapter includes excerpts edited from these papers, mainly the 2026
JFP extension of ETNA, with a major section on evaluation of shrinking from our
under-review work for the Haskell Symposium with George and Leo.

\section{ETNA} \label{sec:etna}

ETNA is, as you may have thought of, named after the famous Sicilian volcano~\cn. The
name follows a convention established by prior work in the fuzz testing community,
Lava~\cite{Lava} and Magma~\cite{Hazimeh:2020:Magma}, both of which have been
instrumental to our design, particularly with the ground truth approach of Magma.

During the design and development of ETNA, we focused on a few key design principles,
centered around usefulness, extensibility, and maintainability.

\subsection{Evaluate for the ground truth, not for proxy metrics.}

How should an evaluation platform measure the effectiveness of a generator? The
software testing literature offers two common answers: \emph{code coverage} and
\emph{mutation testing}. Code coverage is attractive because it is easy to instrument
and compare, but it is at best an indirect signal: higher coverage does not always
translate to better bug finding~\cite{GopinathJG14,EvaluatingFuzzTesting}. For ETNA, we
instead chose mutation testing~\cite{JiaH11}, which measures the effectiveness of a
testing technique by artificially injecting mutations into the system under test and
checking whether testing is able to detect them.

Mutations used in prior evaluation work fall on a spectrum from manually sourced to
automatically synthesized~\cite{Hazimeh:2020:Magma,EvaluatingFuzzTesting,
    FixReverter,TestingNIjfp}. ETNA opts for manual sourcing. This choice makes the
benchmark harder to populate, since every task requires a meaningful mutant and a
property that exposes it, but it also lets us maintain a clearer ground truth: each
mutant should violate some aspect of the property specification. The workloads,
properties, and injected bugs used in this chapter are detailed in
\S~\ref{etna:workloads}.

\subsection{Use minimal, but precise interfaces.}

A key challenge during ETNA's development was that the platform needed to gather
comparable data about the testing effectiveness of a wide variety of existing PBT
frameworks, each of which reports such data in an ad-hoc and non-standardized manner.
Most frameworks report the number of inputs generated before a counterexample is found.
Far fewer expose timing statistics, and none of the frameworks we studied initially
offered a detailed breakdown of generation, testing, and minimization time. Similarly,
most frameworks report the number of inputs that fail to satisfy a precondition as
discards, while others, such as Rust's QuickCheck and Racket's RackCheck, do not
separate discards from passing tests.

These differences matter because ETNA is not evaluating a single library in isolation;
it is trying to compare workloads, strategies, frameworks, and languages. To make those
comparisons meaningful, we settled on a small set of metrics that are easy to measure
across frameworks and on a precise output format that implementations can validate
themselves: JSON conforming to a schema exposed by the ETNA
protocol.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/alpaylan/etna-cli/blob/main/PROTOCOL.md}}
The resulting interface is intentionally modest. It does not try to encode every
feature of every PBT library. Instead, it records the information that ETNA needs to
run experiments reproducibly and compare their outcomes.

\subsection{Every ETNA capability should be available to use manually.}

ETNA acts as an orchestration mechanism: it invokes testing tools, toggles mutations,
parses results, and performs analysis over the collected data. Such orchestration is
fragile even when every component is individually well engineered, because ETNA sits
between loosely coupled systems that were developed independently. In practice, any
opaqueness in this process can make failures difficult to diagnose and, in the worst
case, unrecoverable.

The final guiding principle of ETNA is therefore transparency. Each step of an
experiment should correspond to an operation that a user can understand and, when
needed, reproduce manually. This principle shaped the transition from the initial
library implementation to the standalone command-line tool described in the next
section. Rather than hiding build steps, mutation activation, test execution, and
analysis behind a single opaque pipeline, ETNA exposes them as ordinary commands. That
design makes the platform easier to debug, easier to extend to new languages, and
easier to trust when an experiment produces surprising results.

\section{ETNA Tools}

Adhering to the principles outlined in \S~\ref{sec:etna}, I have built a set of tools
that allow for easier experimentation. The first is the
\code{etna-cli}~\cite{etna-cli}, a tool that allows for managing ETNA
\emph{experiments}, standalone version-controlled units that hold \emph{tests} that
mark the set of \emph{tasks} and the corresponding parameters such as timeout, number
of trials, or any other runtime injected parameter to the running test. Experiments are
created by \emph{etna experiment new}, reproduction and collaboration is as seamless as
possible, a user can upload the automatically committed repository to a version control
tool such as Github, the collaborator can clone the repository; run \emph{etna
    experiment register} to start tracking the experiment.

Another importance concept is a \emph{workload}, a workload is a program with a set of
injected bugs; and properties that define correctness for some behaviors of the
workload. Bugs are paired with the subset of properties they invalidate to constitute
\emph{tasks}, targets for the property-based testing tools to evaluate on. Each ETNA
workload is hosted on its own Github repository, and the \code{etna-cli} hosts an
index~\cite{etna-index} to all currently validated workloads.

Workloads, in addition to defining tasks for the PBT tools, define a set of
\emph{steps} for configuring, building, and running the workload. The
\code{schemas}~\cite{etna-schemas} section in the \code{etna-cli} repository define the
shape of this schema as well as the expected shape of the reporting mechanism for the
results and the persistent messaging format for \emph{capabilities}. Currently, ETNA
workloads have four possible capabilities: \emph{solve} for running a full testing
campaign for a given \emph{testing strategy}; \emph{sample} for sampling a random
generator; \emph{test} for testing a given set of inputs against the strategy
(sample-test pairing is used in the cross-language experimentation in \todo{ref
    section}); and finally \emph{shrink} for shrinking a given input with a shrinking
algorithm.

The testing strategies can be across \emph{frameworks}, such as implementing the same
generator in Hedgehog~\cite{Hedgehog} and QuickCheck~\cite{quickcheck} as we have done
in \S~\ref{evaluating-shrinking}, or within frameworks such as comparison of derived
and bespoke generators in Rocq's QuickChick~\cite{Pierce:SF4} as we have done in
\S~\ref{evaluating-generators-rocq}. ETNA workloads typically have an entry point that
acts as a simple CLI for setting the current property and strategy; adding a new
strategy or property is as simple as adding the required functionality to the workload
and updating the entry point, although the design is flexible as the procedure for
running a workload is ultimately defined in the \emph{steps} file.

Once the user designates a task to solve, they define the strategy, the timeout, the
number of trials and any runtime parameters, all of which constitute a \emph{test}. The
users can bundle tests together into a test file, and run them with additional settings
we have found useful in our own experiments. \code{--short-circuit} tells the system to
only run a given test until the first \emph{failure to solve}, meaning that either the
predefined maximum budget within the strategy was exhausted, or ETNA terminated the
test with an external timeout. \code{--parallel} allows running multiple tests in
parallel, which is especially useful as many PBT tools are single threaded so multicore
execution enables faster experimentation with minimal interference.

The test is ran via the command \code{etna experiment run --tests <test-name>}, after
which ETNA reads the workload running procedure from the provided steps file; runs the
configuration, \emph{activates the mutation}, builds the project, and runs the test for
the given number of trials. One point I am yet to talk about is how the mutations are
defined and how are they managed, which I will now do before diving into the workloads
in \S~\ref{etna:workloads}.

Workloads encode mutations directly in the source language using a comment syntax
recognized by ETNA's mutation management tool~\cite{marauders}. For example, the
following Haskell implementation of binary-search-tree insertion contains both the
correct implementation and a mutant named \code{insert_duplicate_entries}.

\begin{code}[language=Haskell,emph={insert,Node,Leaf,otherwise},emph={[2]k,v,l,r,k',v'}]
insert k v Leaf = Node Leaf k v Leaf
insert k v (Node l k' v' r)
  {-! -}
  | k < k' = Node (insert k v l) k' v' r
  | k > k' = Node l k' v' (insert k v r)
  | otherwise = Node l k' v r
  {-!! insert_duplicate_entries -}
  {-!
  | k < k' = Node (insert k v l) k' v' r
  | otherwise = Node l k' v' (insert k v r)
  -}
  {- !-}
\end{code}

The uncommented base implementation preserves the search-tree invariant by replacing
the value when the inserted key is already present. When ETNA activates
\code{insert_duplicate_entries}, the marked mutant body replaces the base code with an
implementation that treats equality like the right-subtree case, thereby allowing
duplicate keys. In general, a variation begins with a language-specific marker comment,
contains the base implementation, then lists one or more named mutants, each with a
header and a marked replacement body. Since the markers are comments, the workload
continues to compile normally when no mutant is active.

I have also spent time carrying this mechanism further by enabling multiple forms of
mutations, and conversions between them. Today ETNA can use diff patches,
C-Preprocessor macros, match-and-replace mutations, and finally in-AST mutations that
can reduce the experimentation time by removing the compilation requirement, which is
currently only implemented for Rust.

\section{Workloads} \label{etna:workloads}

The initial ETNA workload suite was drawn from application domains that are both
practically relevant to functional programming and common in the PBT literature. Not
every experiment in this chapter uses every workload: the Haskell and Rocq experiments
focus primarily on binary search trees, red-black trees, lambda calculi, and
information-flow control, while the later OCaml and cross-language experiments reuse
the portable subset of these workloads. The complete workload definitions, including
their properties and associated mutants, are hosted in the ETNA workload repositories
indexed by \code{etna-cli}~\cite{etna-index}.

\paragraph*{Data Structures} The first workload, binary search trees (BST), is a small but
useful baseline because BST generation appears repeatedly in property-based testing
papers. In particular, John Hughes's \emph{How to Specify It!}~\cite{Hughes2019HowTS}
uses BSTs as an extended example for writing QuickCheck properties. ETNA's BST workload
ports the mutations and properties from that example, including operations such as
insertion, deletion, lookup, union, and validity checking.

The second workload, red-black trees (RBT), keeps the same broad data-structure domain
but adds a substantially richer invariant. Red-black trees combine ordering constraints
with balancing and coloring constraints, making insertion and deletion easy places to
introduce subtle implementation mistakes. RBTs have also been used in prior PBT
work~\cite{Luck,RuncimanNL08,MistaR19B,Klein09}. ETNA's RBT workload combines BST-like
mutants with additional mutants targeting balancing and coloring errors.

\paragraph*{Lambda Calculi and Type Systems} The third workload is a De Bruijn-indexed
implementation of the simply typed lambda calculus (STLC) with booleans. Generating
well-typed lambda terms is a long-running benchmark problem for
PBT~\cite{PalkaAST11,MidtgaardNPFH17}; the STLC mutations used in ETNA are drawn from
the relevant fragment of a System F case study and mainly exercise mistakes in
substitution, shifting, and lifting~\cite{CombinatorialPBT}.

The fourth workload extends this line to System~F$_{<:}$, following the fuller case
study of \citet{CombinatorialPBT}. Adding polymorphism and subtyping makes the workload
large enough to expose more complex type-manipulation errors, including type
substitution, shifting, and lifting. Bespoke generators for System F terms have also
been studied in recent PBT work~\cite{CombinatorialPBT,BlockchainTesting}, and the same
ideas can be extended to the subtyping variant.

\paragraph*{Parsing} The fifth workload is a parser and pretty-printer for Lu, a language
based on Lua whose implementation was drawn from a Haskell course at the University of
Pennsylvania. Its main property is a round trip: printing a valid Lu expression and
then parsing the printed text should recover the original expression. The released
workload includes a bespoke generator for valid Lu expressions.

\paragraph*{Security} The sixth workload is information-flow control (IFC), based on the
case study from \citet{TestingNoninterferenceQuickly,TestingNIjfp}. The workload tests
whether low-level monitors for abstract machines enforce noninterference: differences
in secret data should not become publicly visible through execution. Its mutants
systematically weaken security checks or taint-propagation rules, yielding tasks that
are especially useful for evaluating feedback-guided generation strategies.

\section{Experiments}

\subsection{Analysis and Presentation}

Before presenting the individual experiments, I will fix the common measurement and
presentation conventions used throughout this chapter. ETNA supports customizable
experiments, but the experiments here use a standard setup unless explicitly noted
otherwise: each strategy is run on each task for a fixed number of trials, usually 10,
with a 60-second timeout. A strategy \emph{solves} a task when it finds the injected
bug within the timeout in every trial. Multiple trials account for the nondeterminism
of random generation strategies, and aggregate measurements are simple averages unless
a figure or paragraph says otherwise.

\begin{figure}
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=.95\textwidth]{figures/etna/experiments-legend.png}
    \\[1.5ex]
    \includegraphics[width=.72\textwidth]{figures/chartkey.png}
    \caption[ETNA task-bucket chart presentation]{ETNA task-bucket chart
        presentation. The upper chart shows one strategy/workload result split across
        timing buckets; the lower key explains the bucket shading convention.}
    \label{fig:etna-presentation}
\end{figure}

Raw averages are not always the right way to communicate these results. If one strategy
takes two seconds on average and another takes three, the practical difference depends
on which bugs are found, how quickly they are found, and which tasks remain unsolved. I
therefore use \emph{task-bucket charts} as the main visual representation. A row in
such a chart groups tasks by the time required to solve them: the darkest bucket
denotes tasks solved within 0.1 seconds, and the remaining buckets denote tasks solved
within 1, 10, and 60 seconds. The lightest bucket denotes tasks that were not solved
within the timeout. For example, Figure~\ref{fig:etna-presentation} shows a
strategy/workload result where 14 tasks are solved almost immediately and 4 tasks
remain unsolved.

Some comparisons need more detail than a task-bucket chart alone can show. In those
cases, I either use alternate bucket definitions, such as counting tasks solved in at
least one trial, or supplement the bucket chart with statistical comparisons. When a
figure departs from the default ``solved in every trial'' interpretation, its caption
states that explicitly.

\subsection{Evaluating Random Generation}
\subsubsection{Rocq}\label{evaluating-generators-rocq}

Rocq gives a different experimental landscape from Haskell. Instead of comparing
multiple mature PBT frameworks, the comparison is mostly within a single framework:
QuickChick~\cite{LeoThesis2018}. Besides the type-based and bespoke generators that
also appear in Haskell experiments, QuickChick supports two strategies that are
especially relevant to ETNA: specification-driven generation, which derives
correct-by-construction generators from inductive
preconditions~\cite{GeneratingGoodGenerators}, and coverage-guided fuzzing, which
combines type-based generation with input mutation guided by branch
coverage~\cite{FuzzChick}.

Both prior evaluations left useful gaps for ETNA to fill. The specification-driven
generation work measured generator throughput on a small IFC example, but did not
measure bug-finding effectiveness. FuzzChick was evaluated on a more realistic IFC
workload with systematically injected monitor bugs~\cite{TestingNIjfp}, but its
performance on other workloads, and against specification-driven generation, had not
been studied in a shared setting.

\paragraph*{Comparing Fuzzers, Derived Generators, and Handwritten Generators} The first
Rocq experiment asks how QuickChick's newer strategies compare with the more
established type-based and bespoke approaches. We use the BST, RBT, and STLC workloads,
plus the IFC case study from the FuzzChick paper. For the first three workloads,
inductively defined specifications are available, for example in Software
Foundations~\cite{Pierce:SF4}, so we can compare type-based, specification-derived,
fuzzing, and bespoke strategies. For IFC, suitable inductive specifications do not
exist, so the specification-derived strategy does not apply; the comparison instead
includes the type-based generator, the type-based fuzzer, a bespoke generator, and the
variational fuzzer used in the original FuzzChick work.

Figure~\ref{fig:coq1} visualizes the Rocq comparison using ETNA task bucket charts. The
simple BST workload establishes a baseline: all four applicable methods solve most
tasks quickly, with the type-based fuzzer falling short on a small number of tasks. The
more interesting pattern appears in RBT and STLC, where the preconditions are much
sparser. In RBT, the type-based generator fails to solve 23 tasks within the 60-second
timeout, and the type-based fuzzer fails to solve 25. By contrast, the bespoke
generator solves all tasks in under ten seconds, while the specification-derived
generator solves all but 10. STLC shows the same broad pattern: precondition-driven
methods substantially outperform type-driven methods.

The IFC workload sharpens the story. Noninterference has a very sparse precondition, so
the type-based generator is essentially unable to generate useful valid inputs. The
bespoke generator is the clear winner, but fuzzing still matters: both the type-based
fuzzer and the variational fuzzer reach parts of the input space that the pure
type-based generator does not. They are not reliable in the same way as the bespoke
generator; over 10 runs, roughly 30 IFC tasks are solved at least once by fuzzing, but
fewer than 10 are solved in every trial.

This variance is important. Figure~\ref{fig:IFC-fine} shows that, if we include tasks
solved in at least one run rather than tasks solved in every run, fuzzers outperform
the pure type-based generator. Fuzzers can get stuck following paths that do not expose
the injected bug, but they can also discover paths that naive generation is unlikely to
reach at all. For IFC, mutation is also cheaper than fresh generation: the type-based
generator spends about four times longer per input than the type-based fuzzer.

\paragraph*{Validating and Improving Fuzzers} These results support the main conclusion of
the FuzzChick work: feedback-guided generation is promising, especially for sparse
preconditions, but it still trails precondition-driven generation when good bespoke or
specification-derived generators are available. ETNA also made it possible to improve
FuzzChick by evaluating it across several workloads rather than only its original IFC
case study.

We focused on two aspects of fuzzing, size and feedback. FuzzChick's original size
schedule started small but quickly moved to large inputs, following the usual
``combinatorial advantage'' intuition that large inputs contain many smaller behaviors.
The BST size experiment described later in this chapter shows why that intuition can
fail: large inputs can make dependency-sensitive bugs harder to expose. Switching to a
more gently increasing size bound improved throughput and, in turn, bug-finding
performance.

The second improvement came from the feedback mechanism. FuzzChick, like
Zest~\cite{Zest}, maintains separate seed pools for valid and invalid inputs. Running
FuzzChick across ETNA's workloads exposed a bug that saturated the invalid-input seed
pool with uninteresting inputs. This problem had been hidden in the original IFC
evaluation because the variational fuzzer can quickly construct valid input pairs and
therefore prioritizes the valid pool. The tuned version of FuzzChick outperformed the
original across the BST, RBT, and STLC workloads, and it is the version used in the
comparison above. Figure~\ref{fig:fuzzercomp} shows the impact of these tuning changes
across the non-IFC Rocq workloads.

\paragraph*{Hardening QuickChick} ETNA also stress-tested QuickChick in ways that
occasional user-level testing had not. One bug came from the main fuzzing loop, which
is written in Gallina with a natural number fuel argument and then extracted to OCaml
integers for efficiency. The Gallina code is structurally recursive, but after
extraction the pattern match on the fuel was translated through an eliminator in a way
that the OCaml compiler no longer recognized as tail recursive. Large ETNA fuel values
therefore produced stack overflows. The fix was syntactic but important: remove the
extra level of indirection in the extracted loop so OCaml could recover tail recursion.

\subsubsection{OCaml}\label{evaluating-generators-ocaml}

OCaml presents a different comparison point from Rocq. Instead of working mostly within
one mature framework, OCaml programmers can choose between several PBT tools:
QCheck~\cite{QCheckOCaml}, which offers a standard QuickCheck-like API; Crowbar, which
wraps random generation and AFL-style fuzzing~\cite{Crowbar}; and Base\_quickcheck,
which is part of the Jane Street Core ecosystem~\cite{base_quickcheck}. For this
experiment we ported the BST, RBT, and STLC workloads to OCaml. Since these frameworks
do not provide the same automatic type-based or specification-based generator
derivation facilities used in the Haskell and Rocq experiments, we ported both
type-based and bespoke generators to each framework.

\paragraph*{Controlling for Size} The earlier experiments already show that generated input
size can significantly affect bug-finding performance. The three OCaml frameworks also
expose quite different default size distributions, so the comparison had to control for
them. Base\_quickcheck's default distribution was similar to the effective size
distributions used elsewhere in ETNA, so we left it unchanged. For QCheck, we avoided
distributions that were heavily bimodal or that generated integer ranges too large to
expose collision-sensitive bugs, using QCheck's smaller-range integer generators
instead. For Crowbar, the default list generator produced very small lists in practice,
so we reimplemented a longer-list generator using Crowbar's API.

Figure~\ref{fig:ocamlcomp} shows the resulting bucket charts. Across these workloads,
Base\_quickcheck with bespoke generators outperforms the other framework/strategy
combinations in nearly all cases. Crowbar with the best bespoke generators we could
write through its interface performed worst among the tested strategies, but this
should not be read as a general claim that Crowbar is less effective as a framework.
The lesson is closer to the FuzzChick result: when effective bespoke generators already
satisfy a property's precondition by construction, coverage-guided fuzzing can add
overhead for little additional bug-finding benefit.

\subsubsection{Cross-Language}\label{evaluating-generators-cross-language}

The experiments so far compare frameworks or generation strategies within a single
programming language. That does not cover all common PBT practice: a generator written
in one language can be used to test a system in another. For example, a
specification-derived generator for well-typed System~F terms written in Rocq was used
to test a higher-order blockchain language implemented in
OCaml~\cite{BlockchainTesting}, and bespoke generators for file-system interactions
written in Erlang were used to test Dropbox's Python-based synchronization
service~\cite{MysteriesOfDropbox2016}.

To support this style of experiment, ETNA decouples input generation from property
execution. A generation strategy outputs serialized inputs together with the time
required to generate each one. On the property side, a runner for a workload reads
serialized inputs and checks the relevant properties. ETNA can then analyze generation
time, execution time, and bug-finding outcomes without forcing all of those costs into
one framework-specific measurement.

\subsection{Evaluating Shrinking}\label{evaluating-shrinking}

The ETNA paper evaluates how well generators find injected bugs, but PBT users also
care about what happens after the first failing input is found. Random generation often
produces counterexamples that are too noisy to debug directly: for example, a
binary-search-tree insertion bug may first appear in a thirty-node tree even though a
one-node tree already exposes the same fault. Counterexample minimization, or
\emph{shrinking}, is the phase that turns such a failure into something usable.

Shrinking is rarely evaluated quantitatively. The dominant evaluation practice in PBT
papers is to report bug-finding performance, code coverage, mutation score, or case
studies, while the shrinking phase is either assumed to work or evaluated with ad hoc
examples. In the ShrinkingEval work, we extended ETNA with shrinking measurements and
used it to compare three Haskell PBT frameworks: QuickCheck, Hedgehog, and Falsify. The
comparison spans the four Haskell ETNA workloads available in that experiment suite:
Binary Search Tree (BST), Red-Black Tree (RBT), Simply Typed Lambda Calculus (STLC),
and System $F_{<:}$. The main question is not whether shrinking is useful, but which
trade-offs different shrinking designs expose.

\subsubsection{Shrinking Designs}

Shrinking has a simple operational shape. Once a generated input falsifies the
property, the framework proposes smaller candidates, tests whether each candidate still
reproduces the failure, and repeats until no tested candidate makes further progress.
These searches usually find a local minimum, not a guaranteed global minimum, so the
organization of the shrink space and the order in which candidates are explored matter.

QuickCheck represents the traditional \emph{structural} or \emph{external} approach.
Users provide a generator and a shrinker separately through
\code[language=Haskell]{Arbitrary}:

\begin{code}[language=Haskell]
class Arbitrary a where
  arbitrary :: Gen a
  shrink    :: a -> [a]
\end{code}

The shrinker operates directly on the generated value. Internally, QuickCheck builds a
lazy rose tree of shrink candidates and searches it greedily, left-to-right, descending
into the first candidate that still fails. This design gives the user direct control
over structural simplification, but it also means that generator invariants must be
reimplemented in the shrinker. A generator may produce only valid trees, well-typed
terms, or API-constructed states, while a generic structural shrinker can still step
outside that valid subset.

Hedgehog and Falsify represent two forms of \emph{integrated} or \emph{internal}
shrinking. In integrated shrinking, shrinking behavior is attached to the generator and
the randomness consumed by the generator, rather than to a separate post-hoc function
over values. Hedgehog generators directly produce shrink trees; Falsify uses
\emph{SampleTrees}, a lazy tree of random samples whose shrunk variants are replayed
through the same generator. This design can preserve generator invariants by
construction and removes the burden of writing separate shrinkers, but it also changes
the shrink space: a generator's control flow and random choices can constrain what
smaller values are reachable.

\subsubsection{Metrics and Setup}

We evaluate shrinking along five axes. First, ETNA still records \emph{bug-finding
    time}, the time spent before the first failing execution. Second, it records
\emph{shrinking time}, the wall-clock time spent after the failure is found. Third, it
records the final shrunk counterexample and compares it to a ground-truth minimum. To
obtain those ground truths, we run LeanCheck's enumerative testing
strategy~\cite{Matela2017ToolsFD,leancheck}; for the tasks where enumeration finds a
minimum, we compute tree edit distance to that minimum using the Zhang-Shasha
algorithm~\cite{zhang1989simple} as implemented by the \texttt{zss} Python
package~\cite{zssPythonGithub}. Fourth, we measure counterexample size. Fifth, we
report time per unit of shrinking progress: shrinking milliseconds divided by the tree
edit distance reduced.

The statistical analysis treats each ETNA task as a paired data set. Trial-level
measurements are collapsed to per-task medians. For each workload, generator family,
and metric, we run a Friedman test followed by Holm-corrected Wilcoxon signed-rank
post-hoc tests, following Dem\v{s}ar's recommendations for comparing algorithms over
multiple data sets~\cite{demsar2006statistical}. Tasks with no successful failure for
one of the compared strategies are excluded from paired time comparisons rather than
assigned an arbitrary penalty; bucket charts still show those tasks explicitly as
unsolved.

For BST and RBT, we evaluate three generator families: a naive type-based generator
that rejects invalid trees, an API-based generator that builds trees by inserting
key-value pairs, and a direct correct-by-construction generator. For STLC and $F_{<:}$,
we evaluate type-based and correct-by-construction generators. The QuickCheck versions
use generic structural shrinkers derived with
\texttt{generic-random}~\cite{genericRandom}; Hedgehog and Falsify use their default
integrated shrinking behavior. Where useful, we also include idiomatic Hedgehog or
Falsify variants of the BST correct-by-construction generators.

\subsubsection{Shrinking Effectiveness}

Figure~\ref{fig:shrinking-ted-to-gt} reports the post-shrink tree edit distance from
each strategy's final counterexample to the LeanCheck ground-truth minimum. The plots
are cumulative count plots: farther up and left is better, while a long tail to the

Type-based campaigns show relatively little shrink movement across all four workloads.
BST/RBT validity and STLC/$F_{<:}$ well-typedness are sparse preconditions, especially

smaller valid inputs. The measured improvement supports this explanation: for
type-based generators, shrinking reduces the tree edit distance to ground truth by a
median of only 3--9 edits per workload, far below the API-based and

The API-based and correct-by-construction campaigns are more workload-dependent. On

\subsubsection{Discussion and Limitations}

The central lesson is that shrinking should be measured separately from bug-finding.
Bug-finding searches for an unknown bug that may not exist; after a failure is found,
shrinking searches a finite neighborhood around a known failing input, and the
acceptable failure mode is simply a non-minimal but still useful counterexample. That
distinction changes how we should interpret cost. It can be reasonable to spend more
time minimizing a known failure than to spend that same time on random search, but it
is less compelling to make bug-finding worse in exchange for a shrinking benefit that
may not materialize.

Across these ETNA workloads, QuickCheck-style structural shrinking remains highly
competitive. It is often faster, and it often reports counterexamples at least as close
to known minima as the integrated shrinkers. This is not a general argument against
integrated shrinking. Hedgehog and Falsify remove the need to write separate shrinkers
and can preserve generator invariants by construction; those usability benefits are
real, but they are different from the performance and counterexample-quality metrics
measured here.

Sample-efficiency helps explain part of the trade-off. A shrinker is sample-efficient
when many of the candidate inputs it tests still reproduce the failure and therefore
make useful progress. For correct-by-construction generators, Hedgehog preserves a
26--56\% failure rate across shrinking steps, compared to 5--12\% for QuickCheck and
roughly 2--3\% for Falsify. Thus, integrated shrinking can avoid invalid candidates
introduced by structural shrinking, but integration alone does not guarantee high
sample-efficiency. In these workloads, Hedgehog's higher failure rate during shrinking
also does not translate into consistently better final counterexamples or shrinking
time.

There are several threats to validity. The generators are based on ETNA's original
QuickCheck generators, which may favor QuickCheck despite our attempts to add idiomatic
Hedgehog and Falsify variants. Measuring performance in Haskell requires careful
strictness in the runners, and different strictness choices could shift wall-clock
measurements. The libraries' budget parameters count different events, making
constant-effort shrinking comparisons hard. Finally, tree edit distance to a
ground-truth minimum is only a proxy for debugging usefulness. It treats symmetric
counterexamples differently, depends on the enumeration strategy used to find the
reference input, and may be closer to the structural view of values used by QuickCheck
than to the randomness-buffer or choice-tree search spaces used by integrated
shrinkers.

\subsubsection{Related Work}

The closest prior work is the study of the Hypothesis
reducer~\cite{HypothesisShrinking}, which evaluates test-case reduction using case
studies, mean input size, and the number of executions in the shrinking phase. The
ShrinkingEval work extends that perspective by measuring distance to known minima,
separating bug-finding time from shrinking time, and comparing several Haskell
libraries and generator families on the same ETNA workloads.

Shrinking is also part of the broader family of test-case reduction techniques. Delta
debugging introduced iterative simplification of failing
inputs~\cite{zeller2002simplifying}, and later work exploited hierarchical
structure~\cite{misherghi2006hdd}, formal syntax~\cite{sun2018perses}, and
trees~\cite{herfert2017automatically}. Other reducers use
probabilistic~\cite{wang2021probabilistic} or weighted~\cite{zhou2025wdd} search, or
target specific domains such as compiler test
cases~\cite{RegehrCCEEY12,donaldson2021test}, SQL queries~\cite{lin2024sqless}, and
dependency graphs~\cite{kalhauge2019binary}. Adjacent lines of work simplify programs
under different preservation criteria: program trimming preserves analysis-relevant
safety behavior~\cite{ferles2017failure}, while container and program debloating
preserve intended functionality while reducing deployed
code~\cite{rastogi2017cimplifier,heo2018effective}.

Overall, the experience report supports a methodological conclusion for ETNA: shrinking
needs first-class measurements. Final counterexample quality, shrink time, per-edit
cost, sample-efficiency, and generator choice expose different trade-offs, and no
single shrinking architecture dominates all of them.
